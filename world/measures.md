# Nelôxia — Weights, Measures and Money

*The **Viennese imperial system**, inherited across the Habsburg span of the federation and
re-expressed in **pure base twelve with the long hundred**. Unit names are **Latin**, because the
chancery that administered them was.*

## Why this system

Half of Nelôxia is Habsburg ground — Pannonia, the Alpine–Adriatic arm, Dalmatia, Galicia, the
Bukovinan arc, and the Lombard–Venetian plain of the western reach. The Viennese system is what
those lands measured in, and it was **already the most duodecimal system in Europe**: the Vienna
foot was 12 inches of 12 lines, a full **gross of lines to the foot**, before any reform touched it.
Nelôxia did not adopt a base. It regularised one it already had, and then exported it.

The unit names are Latin because the **Kingdom of Hungary conducted its administration in Latin
into the 1840s**, and because Nelôxi's Latin crust is the shared-law layer that made accession
worth it. A chancery that wrote its statutes in Latin wrote its measures in Latin.

**Nelôxia was one of the powers that set the international system, so the international system
divides by twelve.** Decimal metric is the *foreign* system here; states outside the Nelosphere are
the ones converting.

## Length

| Unit | Latin | Equals | Habsburg original |
|---|---|---|---|
| **līnô** | *linea* | the line | Linie |
| **unçô** | *uncia* | 12 līnô | Zoll |
| **ped** | *pes* | 12 unçô = **çent līnô** | Fuß — a gross of lines, unchanged |
| **pas** | *passus* | 6 ped | Klafter |
| **ulnô** | *ulna* | the ell | Elle |
| **mīliar** | *milliarium* | **mīl pas** — 1,728 fathoms | Meile, regularised from 4,000 |

The foot needed no reform at all. The mile did: 4,000 Klafter became a clean cube.

## Weight

| Unit | Latin | Equals | Habsburg original |
|---|---|---|---|
| **drakmô** | *drachma* | the dram | Lot |
| **librô** | *libra* | 12 drakmô | Pfund |
| **çentenār** | *centenarium* | **çent librô** — 144 pounds | Zentner, regularised from 100 |

**Two hundredweights, and both are real.** The reformed *çentenār* is 144 pounds because *çent* is
144. The older **deçāgint librô** — 120, the long hundred — survives in timber, herring and hides,
which is the trade it came from. England had exactly this, a 112-pound hundredweight sitting
beside a 100-pound one, for the same reason: bulk trades keep the number that divides.

## Money

| Unit | Latin | Equals | Habsburg original |
|---|---|---|---|
| **kruçār** | *cruciarius* | the smallest coin | Kreuzer |
| **florēn** | *florenus* | **çent kruçār** — 144 | Gulden |

The Austrian gulden was **60 kreuzer** — five dozen — before the reform of the 1850s took it to a
hundred. Nelôxia's reform went the other way, to **144**, the true gross. The pre-reform sixty
survives in the phrase *kvīnkvāgint kruçār* for an old-style price, the way people still quote
guineas.

**Denominations run 1‑2‑3‑6** — twelve's divisors, the dozenal answer to our 1‑2‑5:

- Coins: 1 · 2 · 3 · 6 · `10` (12) · `20` (24) · `30` (36) · `60` (72) kruçār
- Notes: `2` · `3` · `6` · `10` · `20` · `60` · `100` (a florēn) · `300` · `1000`

Three consequences you notice in a shop:

- **There is no 5. Anywhere.** Five divides nothing in twelve; a five-note here is as useless as a
  seven-note is to us. A foreigner reaching for a fiver finds the slot empty.
- **The 3 is the workhorse** — the everyday coin we don't have, because 3 is a third of twelve the
  way 5 is half of ten.
- **The half is the 60**, worth 72.

Shop prices end in **E** and **EE** — 11 and 143, the just-under-round numbers — the way ours end
in 9 and 99. That is where the two extra digits turn up most in ordinary life.

## The misreading, applied to measures

A Nelôxian figure looks foreign and is worth more, and the error is **exactly a fifth per place**
(1.2ⁿ — see `../grammar/12-numbers.md`). This bites on measures as hard as on counts: a cargo
booked as `1000` librô is 1,728 pounds, not a thousand. Six places and the outside world has
under-read by a factor of three.

Nothing is concealed — a Nelôxian tariff schedule is a Nelôxian tariff schedule. It is simply why
every cross-border contract carries a base clause, why conversion is a profession rather than an
arithmetic exercise, and why the clearing houses are worth what they charge.

## Open

- The **currency's name**. Canon has only *rā*, "money." *Florēn* is the unit; what the currency is
  called as a national thing is unwritten.
- Land area: *jugô* (*jugum*, the Joch) is on record as the unit but its dozenal size is not set.
