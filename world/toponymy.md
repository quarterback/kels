# Nelôxia — Toponymy: How Places Get Named
*Worldbuilding reference · charter §73*

> **Try it:** the [city-name roller](../toponyms.html) rolls candidate names on the seven-strategy table below; the [settlements gazetteer](../settlements.html) shows which places are named and which are still open dockets.

> A place-name encodes **who named the place and why**. Different cultures on Nelôxia's map used
> different naming elements, so a town's name reveals which culture was dominant where. This is
> the toponymic equivalent of the four-quarry system: when naming a new place, reach for the
> elements of the culture that would plausibly have named it, rather than defaulting to Finnic.

## The gravity rule (from §64), restated for names

- **The water and the deep Finnic heartland** → native Finnic elements.
- **The Hanseatic ports** → Low German elements.
- **The coast and islands (Norse charting)** → Scandinavian elements.
- **The Slavic frontier interior** → Slavic elements.
- **The Baltic substrate under the mainland** → Baltic (Latvian/Lithuanian) elements, often
  inherited and half-kept by later namers.

Do NOT Finnicise an interior Slavic town or a Hanseatic port. Compose the name from the right
culture's parts.

## How to compose a name (procedure for the founder or a naming agent)

1. **Decide who named it.** Where on the map is it? That fixes the culture (gravity rule above).
2. **Pick the feature.** Is it a fortress, a harbor, a river-mouth, a bay, a market? That fixes
   the element (e.g. fortress → *-bôrk* if German, *-grôd* if Slavic, *-pils* if Baltic).
3. **Pick the modifier.** A descriptor (color, size, a founder's name, a resource) goes in front,
   head-final like all Nelôxi compounds (module 04): *Salz-markõt*, *Nôrd-hafõn*, *Petr-ovô*.
4. **Apply the x=[ks] rule (§47) for official/ceremonial forms** if the town warrants a formal
   register name (as Kunislinnô has the charter-name Kunixa).
5. **Keep foreign scars** (§63): a German element keeps German spelling, a Slavic element Slavic.
   Fix only characters that exist in no language (î, û, â → ô / macrons).

The result: a map you can *read as history* — the naming element alone tells you whether Germans,
Slavs, Norse, Balts, or the Finnic natives were the founders of each place.

# The naming culture — seven strategies, not one formula

> **Try it:** the [city-name roller](../toponyms.html) rolls candidates on the weighted table
> below, by region and era, and gives each one the *story* of why the name exists.

Everything above this line describes **one** way a place gets named: descriptive composition. It
is real, and it is not enough. Compose 250 names that way and every town on the map is a
harbour-fortress, a new-town, or a river-mouth — the naming pattern of a chartered company, not of
a country with centuries behind it.

Real countries name places after **people, events, other cities, saints, misunderstandings, and
outright clerical mistakes**. And after several hundred years of Nelôxian alignment, the cities of
the state would have been *renamed outright* — the way Königsberg became Kaliningrad and Viipuri
became Vyborg — not merely respelled.

## The prohibition (the rule that governs all of this)

**A Nelôxi name must differ from the local name by MEANING or MORPHOLOGY, never by decoration.**

*Milano → Milān* is not a name. *Torino → Torīnô*, *Valga → Valgô*, *Sevastopol → Sevastôpôl* are
not names. They are the same name with diacritics sprinkled on, and they are forbidden. There are
exactly three honest options for any place:

1. **compose a real Nelôxi name** (the strategies below), or
2. **digest the loan with an actual sound change** — cluster simplification, ending swap, calque,
   the x=[ks] rule: *Stettin → Stetīn*, *Danzig → Tantsika*, *Spalato → Spalôt*, or
3. **keep the local name**, tagged *(raw loan)*, which is the gravity principle working as designed.

A *(raw loan)* may carry Nelôxi length-marking (*Lida → Līda*) — that is the tag's whole point. A
name claiming any **other** layer may not.

## The second prohibition — a name is not a description of its town

**What a place is does not decide what it is called.** The trade, the terrain, the freight on the
manifest: none of them has a vote on the suffix.

This is the same failure as the first prohibition wearing better clothes. Let the description
choose the ending and every harbour town ends in *-hafõn*, every foundry says *raud*, every mill
town says *meçā* — and the map reads as a cargo schedule rather than as a country. It is
relexification against the gazetteer instead of against the local name.

Real places contradict themselves constantly. Landlocked towns are called harbour. Inland towns
are called Newport. Towns on flat ground carry a word for mountain, brought by someone who had
just arrived from somewhere that had one. The reason is almost always a person, a saint, a
transfer, or a mistake — and none of those reasons has to agree with the geography.

So: the ground may **suggest** a feature word, and the trade may occasionally **tint** a modifier.
Neither may reach the head. A shipyard town may be a *-bôrk*, a *-mō*, a *-linnô* or a *-kuadje*,
and the name is under no obligation to advertise the shipyard.

## The seven strategies

A strategy is a *mechanism of origin*. The surface form it produces may look like any other kind of
name — that is exactly the point. **Uusatôm** "new harbour" is an *event* name wearing descriptive
clothes: it is what you call the place the year you build it, and it is still the name four hundred
years later.

| # | Strategy | Mechanism | Anchor |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Patron** | named for a person — admiral, general, company director, rector, *posādnik*, founder, local notable. Person + settlement element, a bare family name, or the office rather than the man | **Kunislinnô** (*kunis-* "king's" + *-linnô*) |
| 2 | **Event** | a battle, treaty, wreck, fire, flood, plague, miracle, the market that ran each spring — then **fossilized**: clipped and compounded until the event is no longer audible in it | **Uusatôm** |
| 3 | **Descriptive** | the element composition above. One strategy among seven | **Järvemō**, **Koskenbôrk**, **Fischūsô** |
| 4 | **Transferred** | named for another city in the sphere. Colonists do this constantly; often only the name travels and the sense is lost. Patterns: *Uus-* + homeland, the Romance *Nova +* homeland, or the donor name carried whole | *Nova Trentô* (Valdória) |
| 5 | **Folk-etymology** | the local name **misheard** and re-analysed as Nelôxi words it resembles. Carries a confident *wrong* meaning that nobody questions and the local school teaches | — |
| 6 | **Saint / religious** | the Romance and Orthodox layers: *sant-* / *sent-*, *Sveti-*, *mônt-* + patron | *Santamīk*; Atlanta's *Santsmāra* |
| 7 | **Accident** | the cartographer wrote it down wrong and it stuck. A marginal note — "not known", "see the chart", a blank field, a ditto mark — filed as the name. Two letters transposed in an atlas that outsold its own correction | — |

Plus **Retain**: the local name kept untouched. This must stay a **common** outcome, not a rare
one. The measured coined layer across the gazetteer is genuinely thin — *-sô* appears 7 times,
*-bôrk* 5, *-mō* 2 — and that thinness is correct.

## Weighting — by region, then by era

Uniform weighting is the same mistake as a single formula. The mix follows the place:

- **coastal ports** → more **patron** (admirals, company directors) and **transferred**
- **the interior** → more **folk-etymology**, **descriptive**, and **retain**
- **Romance regions** (the Adriatic, the western arc) → more **saint**
- **the Slavic frontier and eastern corridor** → more **event**
- **the Karelian reserve** → heavily **descriptive**; this is the deep-native layer charter §63
  records as still too thin
- **wildcard, ~14%**: ignore the region's culture entirely and roll a foreign one. A Yemeni
  neighbourhood carrying a Livonian fishing village's name because a captain from there retired
  and it stuck. Some names must simply be strange. (Raised from 4%: see the next section — in a
  state this size, out-of-register names are ordinary, not exceptional.)

Era shifts the weights again: **Hanseatic** (descriptive, saint) → **charter era** (patron, event)
→ **rail and industry** (patron, transferred, functional) → **federal / modern** (administrative,
honorific).

## The name-stock runs as far as the state does

The federation runs White Sea → Riviera → Odessa → Aden, and for centuries it ran further: the
whole northern-Caspian corridor to Herat was **East Neloxia** before it left, amicably, as
Sarmatia (`sarmatia.md`). The Nelosphere still clears through it. A country of that reach does not
have one name-stock; it has everything its people carried home.

So the loan quarries are not the Baltic-and-Romance set alone. Beyond Finnic, Low German,
Scandinavian, Slavic, Baltic, Romance, Yiddish, Pannonian, Danubian, Hellenic, Ottoman, Tatar and
Armenian, the eastern reach is live:

| Quarry | Where it comes from | Sample heads |
|---|---|---|
| **Circassian** (Adyghe) | the Kuban and the Caucasus gate; Sarmatia's grammar lead | *-kuadje* "village", *-hable* "hamlet", *-bgı* "mountain", *-xha* "summit", *-nepk* "river-bank" |
| **Turkic** | Tarun's steppe routes; the corridor's heaviest loan-source | *-orda* "camp", *-jurt* "home-country", *-kent* "town", *-tôbe* "mound", *-bulak* "spring" |
| **Georgian** | Abkhazia, Samegrelo, Guria, Svaneti — the Black Sea's eastern shore | *-kalaki* "city", *-tsixe* "fortress", *-gôri* "hill", *-tskali* "water", *-hevi* "gorge" |
| **Persian** | Herat as the great inland exchange; Sistan and the plateau edge | *-ābād* "settled-place", *-xahr* "city", *-rūd* "river", *-gerd* "round-town", *-bāg* "garden" |

These carry **modifiers, given names and family names** as well as heads — a patron name out of
the corridor is as available as a Hanseatic one.

A name from the far end of the sphere standing in a Livonian county is not an error to be
corrected. It has a reason, and the reason is worth writing down: a corridor family who settled
out of the eastern trade; a garrison rotated home from the eastern stations; a surveyor who named
it in his own words and was not overruled; concession-holders whose word is the one on the deed;
a refugee quarter that outgrew the town. **Nobody local being able to parse it has never once
stopped a name.**

## Endonym and exonym are separate fields

A place has a Nelôxi name **and** a name outsiders use, and they diverge. Canon already records
four distinct mechanisms:

1. **everyday vs ceremonial** — *Kunislinnô* ↔ **Kunixa** (§47, x=[ks])
2. **ledger name vs street name** — **Dūnabôrk / Daugavpils**
3. **endonym vs outsiders' exonym** — *Uusatôm* ↔ **Odessa**; *Marīsô* ↔ Slavic-side **Marzeja** (§30)
4. **route exonyms** — Nelôxi's own names for places beyond the border (*Rīka*, *Vīsbô*, *Saräj*)

Never collapse the two into one field. The gazetteer's own note on Uusatôm — "*Odessa* survives
only as the outsiders' exonym" — is the model.

## Two guards any namer must respect

1. **Orthographic law.** *î û â* exist in neither the source languages nor Nelôxi: they are
   **errors, not fossils**. Only **ô** and the macrons **ā ē ī ō ū**. Foreign scars (*f, sch, z,
   sz, w*) may stay.
2. **§146 sense-collisions.** In a name the sea is **mer**, never the vulgar *merd*; the quay is
   **kājô**, not the sounding-lead *lōd*; the bridge is **pont**, not silk *sildô*. Note also that
   the dictionary's *port* glosses "door" — the toponymic *Portô-* is the Romance crust word.
   Locatable features only take the locative: you can be *at the shore*, not *at the fish*.

The proper-name register is deliberately **exempt** from the §112/§113 lexicon rule (charter
§63/§73). Do not "correct" a name to a canon lexicon form.

## What is decided, and by whom

The roller **proposes**; the founder **decides**. Invented names are not canon until ratified —
the retired "Zephyria Oblast" rename is the standing precedent. Places with no ratified Nelôxi name
are **open dockets**, shown as such in the gazetteer rather than filled with plausible-looking
invention.
